Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the headline numbers and Figure~\ref{fig:curves} the test score of every version along the way. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:all} lists every number.

\paragraph{The guideline as steps beats the guideline in context.}
With no gold data at all, the seed procedures already compete with a frontier model that reads the whole guideline. On Chinese \umr, every closed seed (Jev 33.9, Microsoft 35.0, OpenAI 32.1) is about ten points above GPT-6.1-sol (23.9), although each decision is taken by a model that cannot write text; on English \umr the seeds are within 1.5 points of it (29.0--29.8 against 30.2), the small LLMs as fragment deciders on par (Haiku 28.8). The one seed below the baseline, Haiku on Chinese \umr (20.9), fails in a way that the trace shows at once: it translated every Chinese concept into English (\zh{计划} $\to$ \texttt{plan-01}), which no guideline sentence forbids explicitly (event concepts 30.0 F in the judge, Table~\ref{tab:judge-umr}). On PARSEME, the fragment seeds start well above the frontier baseline in Chinese (GPT-6-luna 62.5, Haiku 59.5, against 50.0), while the closed seeds start low (22.5--37.8), for a reason we discuss below.

\paragraph{Fifty gold sentences of reflection.}
Reflection improves eleven of the twelve chains that finished. On Chinese \umr, Haiku climbs from 20.9 to 33.2 (best 33.6), the first round alone adding 6.9 points by writing the missing convention (``for Chinese the concept is the source word itself, with a sense number''), and Jev from 33.9 to 37.6, the best \umr result, 13.7 points above the frontier baseline. On English \umr the gains are smaller: Haiku 28.8 $\to$ 32.6, Jev 29.8 $\to$ 31.8, Microsoft 29.0 $\to$ 31.0, all above the baseline at $V_{10}$. On PARSEME, the measured closed chains gain most: Jev rises from 37.8 to 63.4 in Chinese and from 22.5 to 53.7 in English, Microsoft from 36.7 to 46.0 and from 25.9 to 43.5. The fragment chains on PARSEME, whose edits are not measured, move less and not monotonically: GPT-6-luna in Chinese peaks at \tbd{} (72.9 at $V_4$), Haiku reaches 62.9 at $V_3$ and drifts back to 53.2 by $V_{10}$ (\S\ref{sec:disc-rsi}).

\paragraph{Against the state of the art.}
On English PARSEME, every procedure after reflection is above the best system of the 1.1 shared task re-scored on the same sentences (Milos, 40.5): Jev at 53.7, Haiku at 58.2, GPT-6-luna at \tbd{} (62.3 at $V_0$), from 50 annotated sentences against the full training data. The English 1.1 systems predate pretrained language models, however, and the frontier model given the guideline is better still (75.6): in English, which these models know best, the guideline in context works, and decomposition with small deciders does not catch up within ten rounds. On Chinese PARSEME the 1.2 systems, trained on the full data with pretrained encoders, remain ahead (TRAVIS-multi 81.1 against our best 72.9). On \umr, our best procedures are 12--18 points below the placeholder for the best shared-task system.

\subsection{Where fragment and closed decisions differ}
\label{sec:case}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.4pt}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{tables/judge_umr}}
\caption{The fine-grained \umr judge (F1 per check, \%) for fragment decisions by Haiku and closed decisions by Jev, seed and $V_{10}$. Selected checks; all 25 are in the released results.}
\label{tab:judge-umr}
\end{table}

Because every unit of an annotation is attributed to the step that made it, the two modes can be compared check by check (Table~\ref{tab:judge-umr}). On \umr the closed procedure wins on what code controls: \emph{granularity} (which words a node covers; Chinese 92.9 against 85.8), \emph{refer} (person and number of pronouns, filled from closed-class tables; 49.0 against 23.2) and temporal relations. The fragment procedure wins on what needs open-ended judgment: the arguments of events (Chinese core arguments 45.6 against 35.7), reentrancies (24.4 against 20.4) and coreference chains (16.4 against 12.7). \jm{} maps nodes through their token alignment, so granularity errors cost twice, and Jev comes out ahead in Chinese (37.6 against 33.2).

In English the order flips (Haiku 32.6, Jev 31.8), and the judge says why: event concepts (72.5 against 55.6) and senses (71.9 against 60.9). The trace shows that most of the difference is not in a decision at all. Of Jev's 212 wrong English event concepts, 51 are inflected forms (\textit{stolen}, \textit{thought}, \textit{fallen}, \textit{trie}) that the seed's rule-based lemmatizer failed to reduce, against 20 for Haiku, which lemmatizes as it writes; Chinese, without inflection, has none. A decision model chooses among the options that code gives it, so the closed mode is exactly as good as its enumerator: when the right concept is not an option, no probability can rescue it. Such an error is cheap to fix once found (an irregular-verb table), and it is found by reading one column of the judge's output.

On PARSEME the PARSEME judge (Table~\ref{tab:judge-parseme}) explains why the closed seeds start so low: they predict 121--175 expressions for 57--59 gold, of which 78--123 are spurious. Every closed question is a test from the guideline's decision trees, answered by a model that has never seen the scheme's sense of ``idiomatic'' or ``light''; at a threshold of 0.5 most candidates pass. Reflection is where the closed procedure learns that sense: Jev's spurious predictions fall from 80 to 9 in Chinese and from 112 to 19 in English, through edits such as ``\zht{有} is not a light verb'', ``no VID for single-character object nouns'' or ``drop IAV on a verb that already has a VPC'', each measured on the training batches before it was kept. What the closed procedure cannot fix this way are expressions no rule proposes (``missed, no candidate'': 11 in Chinese, 16 in English, against 3--7 for the fragment procedures, which may return expressions outside the candidate list).

\subsection{Calibration, cascade and transfer}
\label{sec:res-calib}

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\input{tables/calib}
\caption{Closed procedures with their thresholds as written (raw) and calibrated on the 50 training sentences (cal.); Jev's $V_{10}$ answered by Microsoft-Decision-1; and the cascade to Haiku 5.5 at $\tau=0.7$.}
\label{tab:calib}
\end{table*}

Table~\ref{tab:calib} shows the two closed-mode adjustments of \S\ref{sec:calibration}. \textbf{Threshold calibration} helps the seeds of three of four PARSEME procedures (Jev English 22.5 $\to$ 27.1, Chinese 37.8 $\to$ 39.2, Microsoft English 25.9 $\to$ 29.9) but not the reflected ones: after ten rounds of measured reflection, the thresholds have already been tuned implicitly by the edits that were kept (a ``lower idiom threshold'' edit, for example), and the remaining training gain of calibration (Jev Chinese 88.2 $\to$ 89.6) does not carry over to the test set (63.4 $\to$ 61.4). With 50 sentences, thresholds fitted on the training data overfit quickly. \textbf{The cascade} routes questions Jev answers with confidence below $\tau$ to Haiku. On the training sentences no $\tau$ improved Jev's $V_{10}$ (English 76.7 at $\tau=0$; 64.6, 73.5, 69.6 at $\tau=0.6, 0.7, 0.8$), so a cascade would not have been selected, and at $\tau=0.7$ it also lowers the test score (53.7 $\to$ 44.1). Questions Jev is unsure about are the hard ones, and Haiku, reading a single question without the procedure's history, is not better at them. \textbf{Transfer}: the procedure Jev's chain learned works as well with another decision model. Answered by Microsoft-Decision-1, Jev's English $V_{10}$ scores 44.2, slightly above the procedure Microsoft's own chain learned (43.5): what reflection writes is mostly about the scheme, not about the model.

\subsection{What reflection does}
\label{sec:res-rsi}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.6pt}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{tables/rsi}}
\caption{Ten rounds of reflection: problems the reflector listed, edits tried (with closed-mode retries), edits kept, compressions, and the size of the editable procedure (characters; closed mode includes code).}
\label{tab:rsi}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:rsi} summarizes the ten rounds. The reflector lists 8--11 problems per \umr batch and 2--4 per PARSEME batch, and proposes about one edit per problem. In closed mode, measurement keeps about half of the edits (54 of 109 for Jev on Chinese \umr; 13--16 of 36--46 on PARSEME); a typical rejected edit is right about the batch and neutral elsewhere (``money is quantity'', gain 0.0), or invalid (an edit that broke a YAML file is caught by the loader and never measured). In fragment mode every edit that applies is kept, so the procedure grows faster (Chinese \umr: 36.8k $\to$ 50.8k characters despite 28 compressions) than in closed mode (70.5k $\to$ 73.7k, mostly code), where edits are short and replace text more often than they add it.

The kept edits read like an annotation manual's errata, and they are where the procedure's improvements come from. In Chinese \umr, Jev's kept edits concern discourse connectives (``limit \texttt{and-but}'', ``speech content is not discourse''), the abstract predicates of non-verbal clauses (``\texttt{exist-91} for \zh{X中有Y}'', ``\texttt{have-place-91} only without a verb''), aspect (``add inceptive aspect'') and the person/number of nouns; every one is a readable change to a named step, which an annotator can check and keep or revert. On the training batches themselves, the closed \umr procedures improve steadily: Jev's score on all earlier batches rises from 31.6 after round 1 to 38.9 after round 10, of which 3.7 points carry over to the unseen test documents.
