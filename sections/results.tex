Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the headline numbers and Figure~\ref{fig:curves} the test score of every version along the way. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:all} lists every number.

\paragraph{The guideline as steps beats the guideline in context.}
With no gold data at all, the seed procedures already compete with a frontier model that reads the whole guideline. On Chinese \umr, every closed seed (Jev 33.9, Microsoft 35.0, OpenAI 33.2) is 9--11 points above GPT-6.1-sol (23.9), although each decision is taken by a model that cannot write text; on English \umr the seeds are within 1.5 points of it (29.0--29.8 against 30.2), the small LLMs as fragment deciders on par (Haiku 28.8). The one \umr seed far below the baseline, Haiku on Chinese (20.9), fails in a way that the trace shows at once: it translated every Chinese concept into English (\zh{计划} $\to$ \texttt{plan-01}), which no guideline sentence forbids explicitly (event concepts 30.0 F in the judge, Table~\ref{tab:judge-umr}). On PARSEME, the fragment seeds start well above the frontier baseline in Chinese (GPT-6-luna 62.5, Haiku 59.5, against 50.0), while the closed seeds start low (22.5--37.8), for a reason we discuss below; in English every seed is below the frontier baseline (at most 62.3 against 75.6).

\paragraph{Fifty gold sentences of reflection.}
Reflection improves sixteen of the eighteen chains. On Chinese \umr, Haiku climbs from 20.9 to 33.2 (best 33.6), the first round alone adding 6.9 points by writing the missing convention (``for Chinese the concept is the source word itself, with a sense number''), Jev from 33.9 to 37.6, the best \umr procedure without a cascade, 13.7 points above the frontier baseline, and OpenAI Decisions from 33.2 to 35.6. On English \umr the gains are smaller: Haiku 28.8 $\to$ 32.6, Jev 29.8 $\to$ 31.8, Microsoft 29.0 $\to$ 31.0, all above the baseline at $V_{10}$; OpenAI Decisions (27.6 $\to$ 28.4) stays below it. On PARSEME, the measured closed chains gain most: Jev rises from 37.8 to 63.4 in Chinese and from 22.5 to 53.7 in English, Microsoft from 36.7 to 46.0 and from 25.9 to 43.5, and OpenAI Decisions from 35.1 to 54.5 and from 42.0 to 56.6, the best closed procedure in English. The fragment chains on PARSEME, whose edits are not measured, move less predictably: GPT-6-luna rises in Chinese from 62.5 to 72.5 (best 74.1 at $V_6$), the best Chinese PARSEME result and 22.5 points above the frontier baseline, but in English it stays between 55.3 and 62.3 and ends at 58.4; Haiku reaches 62.9 in Chinese at $V_3$ and drifts back to 53.2 by $V_{10}$ (\S\ref{sec:disc-rsi}).

\paragraph{Against the state of the art.}
On English PARSEME, every procedure after reflection is above the best system of the 1.1 shared task re-scored on the same sentences (Milos, 40.5): Jev at 53.7, OpenAI Decisions at 56.6, Haiku at 58.2, GPT-6-luna at 58.4, from 50 annotated sentences against the full training data. The English 1.1 systems predate pretrained language models, however, and the frontier model given the guideline is better still (75.6): in English, which these models know best, the guideline in context works, and decomposition with small deciders does not catch up within ten rounds. On Chinese PARSEME the 1.2 systems, trained on the full data with pretrained encoders, remain ahead (TRAVIS-multi 81.1 against GPT-6-luna's 72.5), although our best procedure is on par with the third open-track system (MTLB-STRUCT, 73.1) and above the best closed-track one (ERMI, 62.8). On \umr, the best submission to the shared task (Orange/08) scored 36.5 \jm{} in Chinese and 22.2 in English on the full blind test \citep{zeman2026umrst}; our best procedures reach 38.1 (Jev with the cascade) and 32.6 (Haiku, and Jev with the cascade) on chunks of the same test documents, from 50 annotated sentences. The evaluation units differ (five-sentence chunks against whole documents, which carry more document-level relations), so this comparison is indicative, not a re-scoring.

\subsection{Where fragment and closed decisions differ}
\label{sec:case}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.4pt}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{tables/judge_umr}}
\caption{The fine-grained \umr judge (F1 per check, \%) for fragment decisions by Haiku and closed decisions by Jev, seed and $V_{10}$. Selected checks; all 25 are in the released results.}
\label{tab:judge-umr}
\end{table}

Because every unit of an annotation is attributed to the step that made it, the two modes can be compared check by check (Table~\ref{tab:judge-umr}). On \umr the closed procedure wins on what code controls: \emph{granularity} (which words a node covers; Chinese 92.9 against 85.8), \emph{refer} (person and number of pronouns, filled from closed-class tables; 49.0 against 23.2) and temporal relations. The fragment procedure wins on what needs open-ended judgment: the arguments of events (Chinese core arguments 45.6 against 35.7), reentrancies (24.4 against 20.4) and coreference chains (16.4 against 12.7). \jm{} maps nodes through their token alignment, so granularity errors cost twice, and Jev comes out ahead in Chinese (37.6 against 33.2).

In English the order flips (Haiku 32.6, Jev 31.8), and the judge says why: event concepts (72.5 against 55.6) and senses (71.9 against 60.9). The trace shows that much of the difference is not in a decision at all. Jev gets 212 English event concepts wrong against Haiku's 136, and 51 of Jev's are inflected forms (\textit{stolen}, \textit{thought}, \textit{fallen}, \textit{trie}) that the seed's rule-based lemmatizer failed to reduce, against 20 for Haiku, which lemmatizes as it writes; in Chinese, without inflection, both have 107 and almost none is of this kind. A decision model chooses among the options that code gives it, so the closed mode is exactly as good as its enumerator: when the right concept is not an option, no probability can rescue it. Such an error is cheap to fix once found (an irregular-verb table), and it is found by reading one column of the judge's output.

\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.6pt}

\caption{PARSEME error kinds on the test set (counts of gold or predicted expressions): correct; same tokens with the wrong category; overlapping tokens; gold missed because no rule proposed its tokens, or because a proposed candidate was rejected; spurious predictions. F is MWE-based, category-blind as in the official script.}
\label{tab:judge-parseme}

On PARSEME the PARSEME judge (Table~\ref{tab:judge-parseme}) explains why the closed seeds start so low: they predict 121--175 expressions for 57--59 gold, of which 78--123 are spurious. Every closed question is a test from the guideline's decision trees, answered by a model that has never seen the scheme's sense of ``idiomatic'' or ``light''; at the seed's threshold of 0.5, far too many candidates pass. Reflection is where the closed procedure learns that sense: Jev's spurious predictions fall from 80 to 9 in Chinese and from 112 to 19 in English, through edits such as ``\zht{有} is not a light verb'', ``no VID for single-character object nouns'' or ``drop IAV on a verb that already has a VPC'', each measured on the training batches before it was kept. What the closed procedure cannot fix this way are expressions no rule proposes (``missed, no candidate'': 11 in Chinese, 16 in English, against 3--7 for the fragment procedures, which may return expressions outside the candidate list).

\subsection{Calibration, cascade and transfer}
\label{sec:res-calib}

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\input{tables/calib}
\caption{Closed procedures with their thresholds as written (raw) and calibrated on the 50 training sentences (cal.); Jev's $V_{10}$ answered by Microsoft-Decision-1 (on \umr not calibrated, for budget); and Jev with the cascade to Haiku 5.5, at the $\tau$ selected on the training sentences (\umr: 0.8) or, where no $\tau$ helped there (PARSEME), at $\tau=0.7$.}
\label{tab:calib}
\end{table*}

Table~\ref{tab:calib} shows the two closed-mode adjustments of \S\ref{sec:calibration}. \textbf{Threshold calibration} helps six of the eight seeds, a little on \umr (Jev 33.9 $\to$ 34.2 and 29.8 $\to$ 30.2, Microsoft Chinese 35.0 $\to$ 35.8) and more on PARSEME (Jev English 22.5 $\to$ 27.1, Chinese 37.8 $\to$ 39.2, Microsoft English 25.9 $\to$ 29.9), but not the reflected procedures (\umr within $\pm0.2$; PARSEME Jev 63.4 $\to$ 61.4 and 53.7 $\to$ 49.5): after ten rounds of measured reflection, the thresholds have already been tuned implicitly by the edits that were kept (a ``lower idiom threshold'' edit, for example), and the remaining training gain of calibration (Jev Chinese 88.2 $\to$ 89.6) does not carry over to the test set (63.4 $\to$ 61.4). With 50 sentences, thresholds fitted on the training data overfit quickly. \textbf{The cascade} routes questions Jev answers with confidence below $\tau$ to Haiku. On PARSEME it hurts: on the training sentences no $\tau$ improved Jev's $V_{10}$ (English 76.7 without the cascade, 64.6--73.5 with it; Chinese 88.2 against 77.3--82.2), and at $\tau=0.7$ the test score falls too (English 53.7 $\to$ 44.1, Chinese 63.4 $\to$ 53.4), while on the seeds it helps a little (37.8 $\to$ 39.8, 22.5 $\to$ 25.8). Once reflection has tuned PARSEME's rules to Jev's answers, replacing the hard ones with another model's breaks that fit. On \umr, whose closed questions are more open (which concept, which role), the training sentences select $\tau=0.8$ in both languages (Chinese 37.5 $\to$ 37.8, English 38.5 $\to$ 38.8), and the cascade adds a little on the test set too: Chinese 37.6 $\to$ 38.1, the best \umr result, and English 31.8 $\to$ 32.6, level with Haiku's fragment procedure, for \$0.18--0.67 per hundred sentences, still a fraction of Haiku's own cost. On the seeds it adds 1.6 points in both languages (33.9 $\to$ 35.5, 29.8 $\to$ 31.4). \textbf{Transfer.} The procedure Jev's chain learned works with another decision model, and in three of four cases better than the procedure that model's own chain learned. Answered by Microsoft-Decision-1, Jev's $V_{10}$ scores 57.1 on Chinese PARSEME against 46.0 for Microsoft's own $V_{10}$, and 62.0, close to Jev's 63.4, once its thresholds are calibrated for Microsoft, which is the situation calibration is meant for; 36.6 against 35.8 on Chinese \umr; 44.2 against 43.5 on English PARSEME; only on English \umr is it lower (30.4 against 31.0). What reflection writes is mostly about the scheme, not about the model, and Jev's chain learned more of it than Microsoft's.

\subsection{Two rounds with every decider}
\label{sec:small}

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\input{tables/small}
\caption{Every decider after two rounds of reflection (ten training sentences). \umr: 25 test sentences (five chunks, \S\ref{sec:setup}), with the official \jm{} and AnCast++'s sentence-graph F \citep{sun2024ancast}; systems tested on all chunks are re-scored on the same five. \label{tab:small}
\end{table*}

Table~\ref{tab:small} gives every decider the same small budget, two rounds of reflection, and fills the cells the full runs could not reach (GPT-6-luna on \umr). \textbf{A stronger fragment decider} helps most. Claude Sonnet 5.5 has the best fragment seeds and, after two rounds, the best \umr procedure in English (42.5 \jm{}, against 37.1 for GPT-6.1-sol and 38.0 for Haiku at $V_2$) and the best Chinese PARSEME procedure of the paper (78.3, three points below TRAVIS-multi's 81.1, from ten annotated sentences against the full training data); in Chinese \umr it is level with Jev on \jm{} (35.0 against 35.7). English PARSEME stays where every fragment decider leaves it (56--59). \textbf{GPT-6-luna} starts lowest on \umr (Chinese 18.9, English 24.4): like Haiku's seed, it writes English concepts for Chinese words (event concepts 15.7 F in the judge), and two rounds add about ten points in both languages (Chinese 18.9 $\to$ 28.4, English 24.4 $\to$ 34.3), the largest two-round gains in the table. Among the closed deciders, two rounds move \umr by at most two or three points; on PARSEME they matter more for some chains (OpenAI Decisions, English: 42.0 $\to$ 48.6) than for others, and in Chinese the large closed-mode gains of the full runs come only in later rounds (Figure~\ref{fig:curves}).

\textbf{The metric matters.} AnCast++ matches nodes by their content and neighbourhood, \jm{} through their token alignment. On the same predictions the two agree that ten rounds of reflection improve every full \umr chain (\S\ref{sec:results}), though not on some small two-round changes in English, and they disagree on the comparison with the frontier model: AnCast puts GPT-6.1-sol first in both languages (43.3 and 55.9), ahead of Sonnet (41.1, 51.6) and well ahead of the closed procedures (25--32 at $V_2$), while \jm{} puts it among the weakest seeds in Chinese. Over all 114 \umr test results, AnCast is 15.8--20.9 points above \jm{} for the frontier model, 4--6 above on average for fragment procedures and about 3 below for closed ones (\texttt{results/ancast\_vs\_jumatch.json}). The procedures get their alignments from code and the frontier model writes its own, so our advantage over it on \umr is an advantage on the official metric, which rewards well-aligned graphs, and not on graph content alone.

\subsection{What reflection does}
\label{sec:res-rsi}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.6pt}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{tables/rsi}}
\caption{Ten rounds of reflection: problems the reflector listed, edits tried (with closed-mode retries), edits kept, compressions, and the size of the editable procedure (characters; closed mode includes code).}
\label{tab:rsi}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:rsi} summarizes the ten rounds. The reflector lists 9--11 problems per \umr batch and 2--4 per PARSEME batch, and proposes about one edit per problem. In closed mode, measurement keeps about half of the edits (54 of 109 for Jev on Chinese \umr; 13--16 of 36--46 on PARSEME); a typical rejected edit changes nothing measurable (``money is quantity'': no change on the batch or the earlier batches), lowers the earlier batches, or is invalid (an edit that broke a YAML file is caught by the loader and never measured). In fragment mode every edit that applies is kept, so the procedure grows faster (Chinese \umr: 36.8k $\to$ 50.8k characters despite 28 compressions) than in closed mode (70.5k $\to$ 73.7k, mostly code), where edits are short and replace text more often than they add it.

The kept edits read like an annotation manual's errata, and they are where the procedure's improvements come from. In Chinese \umr, Jev's kept edits concern discourse connectives (``limit \texttt{and-but}'', ``speech content is not discourse''), the abstract predicates of non-verbal clauses (``\texttt{exist-91} for \zh{X中有Y}'', ``\texttt{have-place-91} only without a verb''), aspect (``add inceptive aspect'') and the person/number of nouns; every one is a readable change to a named step, which an annotator can check and keep or revert. On the training batches themselves, the closed \umr procedures improve steadily: Jev's score on all earlier batches rises from 31.6 after round 1 to 38.9 after round 10, of which 3.7 points carry over to the unseen test documents.
