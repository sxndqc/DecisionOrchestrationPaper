\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We annotated two complex schemes step by step along their guidelines. Compiled into a procedure of rule nodes and small decisions, a guideline that is too long to follow in context becomes something small and cheap models can follow: seeds written from the guideline alone, with no example, match or beat a frontier model reading the whole guideline on three of four task--language pairs, 8--11 points ahead of it on Chinese \umr on the official metric (on graph content alone, measured by AnCast++, the frontier model stays ahead). Every error has an address, so it can be traced to the step that made it, and a reflector can fix the step: from fifty gold sentences, five at a time, the procedures improved in twelve of fourteen runs, by up to 31 points, and on English PARSEME every reflected procedure is above the best shared-task system on the same sentences; on \umr the best procedures also score above the best shared-task submission's published results (38.1 against 36.5 in Chinese, 32.6 against 22.2 in English), though on smaller evaluation units. Decision models that only return probabilities, at a few cents per hundred sentences, take the best \umr result. The procedure keeps a readable history of what it learned, and the more gold arrives, the more it has to learn from. We think this makes decision orchestration a natural first step for a new annotation project: start from the guideline, annotate with cheap models, and let the procedure improve as the first gold comes in.

\section*{Limitations}
\textbf{Scale.} Test sets are 95--100 sentences per task and language and training is 50 sentences; each chain was run once, with one document order. With 57--59 gold expressions per PARSEME test set, one expression is worth about one point of F, so differences of a few points there should not be over-read; we did not repeat chains with other document orders to measure the variance of reflection. \textbf{Unfinished runs.} OpenRouter limits each OpenAI model to 20 requests a minute for our account, and our API budget was fixed. GPT-6-luna as a \umr fragment decider needs about 3,000 calls per test pass, and the OpenAI Decisions chains measure every edit; we therefore ran GPT-6-luna and OpenAI Decisions on \umr, and Sonnet 5.5 as a fragment decider, for two rounds only, tested on 25 sentences (\S\ref{sec:small}); the Chinese OpenAI Decisions runs (two rounds on \umr, the seed test on PARSEME) and the later rounds of the English PARSEME chain did not finish (red dashes in the tables). \textbf{Data editions.} PARSEME 2.0 has no Chinese data and only an English trial set, so we use editions 1.2 (Chinese) and 1.1 (English); the English 1.1 systems predate pretrained models. There is no \umr 3.0 release; we use the 2026 shared-task data, which is based on \umr 2.1, and the shared task's best results are quoted as published, on whole documents, not re-scored on our five-sentence chunks. \textbf{Seeds.} The seeds were written by the authors (with an LLM's help) from the guidelines; their quality affects every result and we have not measured the effort they take. Closed and fragment seeds share the decomposition, so the comparison between modes is not a comparison of decompositions. \textbf{Reflection.} Fragment-mode edits are accepted unmeasured and drift; the same reflector (Claude Sonnet 5.5) was used throughout. \textbf{Models.} All models are commercial services whose behavior may change; we release the predictions, the procedures at every version, and the cached answers that their terms permit.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
We use the \umr shared-task data and PARSEME 1.1 and 1.2 under their licenses (PARSEME 1.2 Chinese: CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) and collect no data from people. The method aims to make the first annotations of a new scheme cheaper and easier to check; it could reduce demand for annotation work, and we see it rather as a way to direct annotators to the decisions that need them. Automatic annotations should be checked before they are used as gold. The runs of this version cost about \$59 in logged API charges (decision services \$13, LLM calls \$46), including runs that were stopped; reflection used a subscription. Generative AI assisted the implementation, the running of the experiments and the editing of this paper; the authors are responsible for all content.
