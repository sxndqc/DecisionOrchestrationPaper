\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We wrote annotation guidelines as procedures that ask a decision model closed questions and learned them from five annotated sentences at a time, keeping only edits that measurably help. Where the guideline is a sequence of tests over candidates that rules can enumerate, as for verbal multiword expressions, sixty annotated sentences and a few dollars give a procedure that beats direct LLM annotation by 5--18 F and, in English, the supervised systems of the shared task. Where the output is mostly structure, as in UMR, the closed interface is the bottleneck: routing and reflection help, but direct generation is as good or better. The procedures, their edit histories and their decision logs are readable artifacts, and they show where a scheme's conventions depart from its guideline, as the aspect inventory did. Future work should let a procedure ask structural questions as reliably as categorical ones, choose the routing threshold on development data, and test the method with annotators in the loop.

\section*{Limitations}
We test two annotation schemes in two languages each, with one run per configuration; repeated runs and other document orders would show the variance of reflection, which we only bound for decision noise. The Chinese \umr test set had been inspected in earlier analyses of this project, so its comparisons are retrospective; the English \umr and both PARSEME test sets were not used before the final runs, except that the seed's candidate coverage was computed once on the Chinese PARSEME test set, without changing the seed. PARSEME English comes from edition 1.1, whose systems predate pretrained encoders; our comparison with them shows data efficiency, not a new state of the art, and edition 1.2 open-track systems remain far ahead in Chinese. The procedure reads the lemmas, parts of speech and dependency relations provided with the PARSEME data. The seed procedures were written by the authors from the guidelines with knowledge of the task, and the earlier \umr procedures used training documents for hand-tuning; their gold exposure is reported with each version. Jev is a commercial service with a version identifier, and LLM behavior may change; we release every version of the procedures, their edit histories with the measured effect of each edit, and the test-set predictions. Costs are list prices billed by one provider at the time of the experiments.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
We use publicly released annotated corpora under the licenses of their releases (e.g., PARSEME 1.2 Chinese is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). No new human annotation was collected and no personal data were processed. Procedures that annotate cheaply could be used to produce training data at scale; their errors are systematic and readable, which makes them easier to audit than those of a direct generator, but outputs should still be checked before they are used as gold. LLMs were used as components of the method, as described, and to assist with code and writing; the authors are responsible for all content.
