\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLMs as annotators}
LLMs can replace or assist crowd workers on many labeling tasks \citep{wang2021reduce,gilardi2023chatgpt,tornberg2023chatgpt,he2024annollm,ziems2024css,tan2024annotation}, but their quality varies by task and must be validated against human labels \citep{pangakis2023validation}. 
% Human--LLM collaboration allocates uncertain items to people or asks people to verify labels \citep{li2023coannotating,wang2024humanllm}.
% When a benchmark has conventions of its own, zero-shot labels drift from them; putting the guideline into the prompt and refining it in rounds of moderation brings LLM annotation closer to the gold \citep{kim2026refining}, and guidelines can also be learned from errors \citep{pang2023guideline} or written as code that a model is trained to follow \citep{sainz2024gollie,wang2023code4struct}. 
Zero-shot LLM labels often miss a benchmark's specific conventions. Prior work narrows this gap by adding guidelines to prompts and refining them through moderation \citep{kim2026refining}, learning guidelines from errors \citep{pang2023guideline}, or training models to follow guidelines written as code \citep{sainz2024gollie,wang2023code4struct}. Yet it remains unclear how a model can apply a long guideline reliably as all of these keep the guideline as one block of context for one model call. 

For complex ontologies such as \umr, whose guidelines run to hundreds of pages \citep{gysel2021design,bonn2025umr2}, that block exceeds what a model reliably uses: performance falls for information in the middle of long contexts \citep{liu2024lost} and drops roughly exponentially with the number of instructions to be followed at once \citep{harada2025instructions}. Expert-level structured annotation by prompting remains weak \citep{ettinger2023expert,sun2024chinese}, and supervised parsers need large corpora \citep{chun2024pipeline,markle2025setup}. Together, these limitations leave a gap: how to apply long annotation guidelines reliably without a large labeled corpus.

\paragraph{Recursive self-improvement and rule-based reflection}
Early NLP systems improved annotation by learning rules from gold labels \citep{brill1992simple,brill1995transformation,ramshaw1995chunking}, but relied on predefined rule templates. LLMs have renewed this idea with more flexible revisions of prompts \citep{pryzant2023protegi,khattab2024dspy}, rule sets \citep{zhang2025ace,zhao2024expel}, or harness codes\citep{zelikman2024stop,hu2025adas,zhang2026dgm}. However, their use in expert structured annotation remains underexplored, especially when annotations must follow long, complex guidelines. The limits of self-correction without external feedback \citep{huang2024cannot} motivate a structured harness that constrains revisions and checks whether they improve annotation accuracy.

Before neural models, the most successful annotation learners in NLP learned readable rules from a little gold: transformation-based learning starts from a simple annotator and greedily adds the rewrite rule that most reduces errors against gold \citep{brill1992simple,brill1995transformation,ramshaw1995chunking,ngai2001fast}; rule induction \citep{cohen1995ripper} and heuristic learning \citep{mitchell1983lex,lenat1983eurisko} similarly propose and refine heuristics and keep those that work on examples; data programming combines labeling functions written by people \citep{ratner2017snorkel}. With LLMs, prompts, programs and agent scaffolds are optimized from feedback \citep{zhou2023ape,pryzant2023protegi,yang2024opro,khattab2024dspy,opsahl2024mipro,yuksekgonul2025textgrad}, by reflecting on execution traces \citep{agrawal2025gepa,shinn2023reflexion,madaan2023selfrefine}, by growing playbooks or rule sets from experience \citep{zhang2025ace,zhao2024expel,zhu2023rules}, or by letting systems rewrite their own code under empirical validation \citep{zelikman2024stop,hu2025adas,zhang2025aflow,zhang2026dgm,novikov2025alphaevolve}. Self-feedback without external signal is unreliable \citep{huang2024cannot}. Our reflection is closest to transformation-based learning: gold annotations, not a model's opinion, decide which edits stay, the learned object is a readable procedure rather than weights, and each round is a small set of rewrites of rules, options and instructions. Unlike TBL's single rule template, the reflector may edit any part of the procedure, and unlike most prompt optimizers, it attributes each error to the step that made it.
