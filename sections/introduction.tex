Annotating a complex ontology takes two things: an understanding of the structure the scheme describes, and the scheme's own codebook, the conventions that make one annotation right and a plausible alternative wrong \citep{kim2026refining}. Early in a project, a new scheme, language or domain has a guideline but little gold. With enough gold, supervised models learn both \citep{bevilacqua2021spring,bai2022graph}; with a handful of sentences they learn neither. Large language models bring the first ingredient for free: they understand sentences, events and referents well enough to bootstrap an annotation. What remains is to give them the codebook.

For simple labeling, the guideline fits into the prompt \citep{gilardi2023chatgpt,tan2024annotation,kim2026refining}. For complex schemes it does not. The \umr guidelines run to more than 300 pages \citep{gysel2021design}; put into one context, the rules that matter for a given sentence sit in the middle of a long document, where models use them least \citep{liu2024lost}, and an annotation must satisfy dozens of them at once, where following degrades with each added instruction \citep{harada2025instructions}. A second problem is just as practical. At the start of a project, people and models correct each other's errors, and correcting an error needs a diagnosis: which rule was misapplied, and where. When the whole guideline sits in one prompt and the model writes the whole annotation, an error cannot be traced to a rule, and fixing it means rewording a prompt and hoping.

We propose \textbf{decision orchestration} (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}): compile the guideline into a procedure of small steps and run them in order. Code (rule nodes) cuts the input, enumerates candidates and assembles the output; models are consulted only at decision nodes, each with the part of the guideline it needs. A decision node is either a \emph{fragment} decision, in which an LLM writes a small schema-checked piece of the annotation, or a set of \emph{closed} questions whose options code has listed, answered with probabilities by a decision model and read by rules with thresholds. Because every output unit comes from a logged step, every error has an address. A reflector LLM uses these addresses to improve the procedure itself from five gold sentences at a time: it lists every problem it finds, edits the responsible steps, and in closed mode keeps only edits that measurably help; we call this recursive self-improvement (RSI) of the procedure, as the model weights never change.

To our knowledge, this is the first work to annotate complex schemes step by step along their guidelines, and we show that it pays twice: (1) errors are diagnosable and can be fixed where they arise, and (2) with very little data, RSI makes the procedure much better than the guideline it started from and than a frontier model given the whole guideline. We use small LLMs (Claude Haiku 5.5, and GPT-6-luna on PARSEME) and, beyond them, fast decision models that output only probabilities (Jev, the OpenAI Decisions API, Microsoft-Decision-1), with a mid-size reflector (Claude Sonnet 5.5). We test on samples of two complex benchmarks, each in Chinese and English: the test sets of the 2026 UMR parsing shared task \citep{zeman2026umrst} and PARSEME verbal multiword expressions \citep{ramisch2018parseme,ramisch2020parseme}, starting from seeds written from the guidelines alone, with no examples. Our findings:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Steps beat the guideline in context.} Without any gold, seed procedures decided by small models match or beat GPT-6.1-sol given the whole guideline; on Chinese \umr every closed seed is about ten points ahead (Jev 33.9 against 23.9 \jm{} F1; \S\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{A handful of gold goes a long way.} Ten rounds of reflection on five gold sentences each improve eleven of twelve procedures, by up to 31 points (Jev on English PARSEME, 22.5 $\to$ 53.7). The best \umr procedure (Chinese, 37.6) is decided by Jev at under 30 cents per hundred sentences, and on English PARSEME every reflected procedure is above the best shared-task system re-scored on the same sentences.
\item \textbf{Errors have addresses.} The step-level judge shows where the two kinds of decision fail: closed procedures lose where the code offers no right option (e.g., an English lemmatizer that leaves \textit{stolen} unreduced), fragment procedures where structure is left to the model; measured edits keep closed reflection from drifting, unmeasured fragment edits do not (\S\ref{sec:case}, \S\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}
These results suggest that LLMs can learn an annotation scheme from its instructions and a few dozen gold sentences, keep improving as new gold arrives, and that the models doing the annotating need not be frontier models: even simple decision models can serve.
